Quasicrystals, however, offer a solution. First discovered in materials science by Dan Shechtman, quasicrystals are aperiodic structures that possess long-range order and rotational symmetries (such as icosahedral symmetry) that are forbidden in periodic crystals. This unique property allows quasicrystalline lattices to approximate continuous isotropy much more effectively than periodic lattices, making them a viable candidate for the substrate of spacetime.

Imagine a perfect tiling pattern. Now introduce a defect where the tiles don't quite match up, forcing a unique configuration around a center point. This center point is the particle.  
To move the particle, the defect must propagate. For the defect to move from point A to point B, the tiles at A must relax back to the vacuum state, and the tiles at B must rearrange into the defect state. This requires a coordinated series of phason flips.

* **Light Particles (Low Mass):** Correspond to defect configurations that require very few tile flips to move a unit distance. The lattice offers little resistance. The geometric "cross-section" of the defect in the internal space is small.

$$M \\propto \\frac{dN\_{flips}}{dx}$$
